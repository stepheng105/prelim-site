PhD Prelim Exam Prep

Central hub for prelim exam preparation across the four tested subjects.

Subject Areas

  • Basic Analysis and Topology — sequences, series, differentiation/integration, general topology, metric spaces
  • Measure Theory — measures, integration, Radon-Nikodym, Lp spaces
  • Complex Analysis — analytic functions, contour integration, residues, conformal mapping
  • Functional Analysis — Banach/Hilbert spaces, bounded operators, spectral theory

Exam Overview

Format & Logistics

  • Content: 8 questions total — two from each subject (real analysis/topology, measure theory, functional analysis, complex analysis).
  • Time limit: 5 hours.
  • Schedule: Offered every September and June, so you get 2 attempts per year (including your first year), and can retake it as many times as you want through the end of your third year.
  • Testing conditions: Starts at 9:00 am, usually in the grad library or colloquium room. Snacks are typically provided beforehand. You'll be given blank printer paper and a sheet of stickers — place a sticker in the top right(?) of each page you want graded; anything unstickered is treated as scratch work and won't be submitted/graded.
  • In-exam resources: No resources allowed besides writing utensils (a watch is fine, as are snacks/drinks). Instructors should be reachable in person or by phone during the exam if you have questions.

Grading & Progression Policy

  • Passing criteria: Varies by year, but roughly 50–60% for a master's-level pass and 75–85% for a PhD-level pass.
  • To advance: You need a PhD pass in at least one subject and a master's pass (or a second PhD pass) in another. Once you've done this, you can shift focus toward research.
    • Bonus: a PhD pass in a subject exempts you from that subject's core sequence (algebra, analysis, manifolds) — great if you're strong in an area already or want to self-study instead of taking the class.
  • If you don't pass in time: Academic probation if you haven't gotten the required passes by the end of year 2; possible release from the program if you haven't by the end of year 3.

General Advice

Preparation Advice

  • Start early and study consistently — one question a day, thoroughly, beats seven questions crammed into one day a week.
  • Memorize your theorems and definitions, especially the core ones per subject. Flashcards, active recall, whatever sticks.
  • Learn to recognize question "types." E.g., a functional analysis question asking you to show an operator is bounded is probably a closed graph theorem question. Recognizing the type gives you a path forward even before you've solved anything.
  • Save every problem you work through and build your own compendium of solutions — a resource to compare against when you revisit the question or hit something similar later.
  • As you get closer to the exam, work full mock prelims (one every weekend near the end is a good pace). Pull a random one from the archive, or target specific concepts. Mimic real conditions as closely as possible: 9:00 am start, no resources, no phone, printer paper, 5-hour limit.

Question author patterns: each subject's 2 questions are written by whoever most recently taught that class. Look for patterns in each professor's style — worth digging through the archive specifically for this (the author filter on each subject page's random generator is built for exactly this).

Exam Day Advice

  • The exam starts at 9:00 am — practice actually getting up and to campus by then in the weeks prior. A mock prelim is a good excuse to test the routine.
  • Eat well the night before and the morning of. Snacks are allowed — bring something that won't distract you. Bring a big water bottle.
  • If you get test anxiety, have a grounding technique ready. Try butterfly tapping while breathing in for 4, hold for 4, out for 4.

In-Exam Logistic Advice

  • 8 questions, 5 hours ≈ 37.5 min/question on average, but questions aren't equal. Sort them into 4 categories: Easy Wins (immediate solutions), Core Competency (familiar concepts), Known Challenges (difficult but approachable), Black Holes (high uncertainty).
  • Do Easy Wins and Core Competency first — good to get points on the board early.
  • Move to Known Challenges next; expect this to eat at least half your time. Use scratch paper freely.
  • Save the last 30–60 minutes to double-check proofs, verify scaffolding, and take a swing at anything you haven't attempted yet.

Partial Credit Advice

  • Write down every relevant definition and theorem.
  • Outline your proof's structure before you start writing it.
  • Structure proofs clearly: claim → proof → conclusion. Correct and readable.
  • Don't erase failed attempts — you have plenty of paper and stickers. A dead-end attempt can still earn partial credit.
  • If you're stuck, work specific cases ("in the $n=0$ case..." or "if the measure space is $\sigma$-finite..."). It can net partial credit and help you get unstuck.
